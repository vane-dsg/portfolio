Redesigning how a state submits debts for collection

I evolved a submission flow into a system where public servants register, track and fix their requests on their own.

  • Public sector
  • Problem solving
My role
UX/UI Designer
Client
Attorney General's Office
Team
1 designer, 1 requirements analyst, 4 devs and 2 QAs
Timeline
2 months

What I did

I redesigned the flows and defined rules, fields and behaviors together with the attorney in charge, the department's coordination and the requirements and development teams.

Context

When someone fails to pay an amount owed to the state (like a fine), the agency that issued the charge can't collect it on its own. That's the job of the Attorney General's Office: it receives and reviews the debt and, if everything is correct, registers it officially so the state can collect it. That's why every public agency has to send its debts there.

  1. Debt at the source agency

    A state agency (such as a department or public authority) issues fines that weren't paid, and the debt has to be sent to the Attorney General's Office to be collected.

  2. Debt request

    We're here!

    The agency's public servant fills in the debt details, the debtors and the charges, and submits the request. Then they track its status and fix it or reply when the Attorney General's Office points out a problem.

  3. Debt management

    The request is either approved automatically or reviewed by the Attorney General's Office. Once registered, the debt is managed and collected by the state.

Proto-personas

Given our timeline, almost everything I knew about these users came from the stakeholders, who closely followed their mistakes and questions.

Public servant at a state agency - who submits

35 to 60 years old · not from the legal field · little digital familiarity

Goal

Submit the right debt and know which stage it's in.

Needs

  • see only the fields that apply to their agency
  • know what's missing before submitting
  • track and fix the request after submitting

Pain points

  • locked fields, or fields that don't apply to their agency
  • no warning about what's missing to move on
  • after submitting, can't see the status or fix mistakes

Debt analyst - who receives and corrects

Works in the debt department of the Attorney General's Office · checks requests from every agency

Goal

Find the errors and get the request ready to be registered.

Needs

  • point out the error to the agency inside the system;
  • receive the fix in the system itself;
  • stop editing the registration by hand.

Pain points

  • opened a ticket for every error;
  • the agency's reply came by email;
  • edited the registration by hand.

Before, both of them relied on tickets and emails to solve any error.

Problem

I went through the submission flow in the old system as if I were a public servant, noting every point where they could struggle and listing possible solutions. What happened after submission was reported by the stakeholders.

In red, the problems. In green, the first solution ideas.

Redesigning the flow

When I presented the solutions to the stakeholders, other questions came up from their requests, the deadline and what was technically feasible. These decisions were negotiated and shaped the final flow.

  1. 01.  Same visual foundation as the management system

    Both systems had to ship fast. So I reused flows, components and behaviors I had already designed for the Management System.

  2. 02.  One registration per request

    This means attaching the same document and repeating the case number for each registration. We chose this because mixing several registrations in one form created conflicts. In return, filling in the form became more linear and intuitive.

  3. 03.  Fields defined by the source agency

    In the Management System, which I also designed, people with permission choose which fields appear in each agency's request form when they register the debt source.

  4. 04.  Request edit history

    Public servants could now request edits, so those edits had to be recorded. Together with the development team, given the deadline and technical feasibility, we decided each edit would become a new request, grouped under the original one with an expand arrow.

  5. 05.  Request status timeline

    Someone suggested a history button next to the status, in a table that already had many columns and a lot of information. I suggested making the status pill itself clickable and leading to the history, without taking up more space.

Solution

The before and after of each step, in submission order.

01  Debt details

Before
  • Locked fields came prefilled with data the system already had. Since they couldn't be edited, they didn't need to be shown.
  • The procedure type was always "registration request", which is exactly what the user is doing.
  • Fields like license plate and chassis number showed up for every agency. The public servant had to know in advance which ones applied to their debt.
  • Attachments were in a separate tab, and the public servant had to read a block of text to know what to attach.

01  Debt details

After
  • Fields with information the system already had were removed from the screen, so the public servant only fills in what's needed.
  • Only the fields defined for the agency appear, configured in the Management System.
  • Attachments became a section of this step, and the document type is picked from a list.
  • The other numbers became two optional fields, number type and number, for when they're still needed.

02  Debtors and charges

Before
  • 14 fields showed up at once, several of them locked with no explanation.
  • It only allowed one contact and one address per debtor, which made collection harder.
  • The public servant registered the debtors in one tab and only linked them to the registration in the next one. They had to know in advance that each debtor could belong to a different registration.
  • The charges shared the screen with the debt details and the link to the debtor.
  • The list of charges didn't show the total, so the public servant couldn't check the final amount.

02.1  Debtors

After
  • The debtor is added in its own modal, with contacts and addresses in separate sections.
  • Each debtor accepts several contacts and addresses.
  • The list shows who has already been added before moving on, and when it's empty the screen says what to do.

02.2  Charges

After
  • Charges got their own step, with the fields inline and the table right below.
  • Add sits next to the charge fields, and Submit request is the primary action.
  • The table shows the total and the number of charges, updated with every addition.

03  Searching requests

Before

After submitting, the public servant saw a list of cases that didn't show which stage their request was in.

03  Searching requests

After

A dedicated search for requests, with each one's status and summary cards at the top.

04  View

Iteration

One screen brings together everything about the request, with the status at the top.

05  Edit/reply

Before

For each error, the analyst opened a ticket, waited for the reply by email and edited the registration by hand.

05  Edit/reply

After

The analyst points out the error, and the public servant gets the notice in the system and by email, with a button that goes straight to the request, where they can edit it or just reply.

Next iterations

What I would change in the next version.

  1. 01.  Debtor in a drawer, with inline contact and address

    The drawer would keep the list of debtors in view and avoid opening one form on top of another to add contacts and addresses.

  2. 02.  Review step before submitting

    A final read-only screen with all the filled-in data and shortcuts to edit, so the public servant could check the whole request before submitting.

  3. 03.  Action required already showing the reason

    With the reason in the message itself, the public servant can decide right away whether they need to edit or just reply, without opening the request.

What I'll measure after launch

  1. 01.  Time to submit a request.
  2. 02.  Tickets and emails opened to register or fix requests.
  3. 03.  Requests sent back with action required.

A state's public debt management system

I built from scratch a system that brings together a state's entire public debt cycle, replacing a legacy system.

  • Design System
  • Complex system
My role
Product Designer
Client
Attorney General's Office
Team
1 designer, 1 requirements analyst, 10 devs and 4 QAs
Timeline
1 year and 2 months

What I did

I was the only designer on the project. I designed the flows, the web and mobile screens for the whole MVP and the design system, along with the request submission system

  • 21Modules
  • ~86User flows
  • ~370Screens (270 web, 100 mobile)
  • 60Design system components

Context

The public debt registry gathers amounts that were not paid to the state and are then collected by the Attorney General's Office. It accounts for an important share of the state's revenue.

How it was

All management ran on a mainframe system, built and maintained by another state agency and split into several terminal applications.

Goal

Replace the legacy with a system owned by the Attorney General's Office, bringing the full debt cycle into one place.

Methodology

The system has many flows, and each one went through the same cycle within the sprints, with meetings twice a week. The deadline was tight, so stakeholders only saw high-fidelity, prototyped screens.

  1. 01

    Planning

    We defined which module would be designed in the sprint.

  2. 02

    Understand the problem

    Meeting with the stakeholders and conversations with subject matter experts.

  3. 03

    High fidelity

    I designed the screens straight in high fidelity, already prototyped.

  4. 04

    Review

    In the next meeting, corrections and change requests to the flow came up.

  5. 05

    Adjustments

    I made the adjustments and brought the new version to the next meeting, until the flow was closed.

The cycle repeated for every module.

My notebook

Diagrams and journey maps weren't an expected deliverable, but I needed them to understand the rules of each module and user story. So I did everything in my notebook, during and after the meetings.

Problem

What made managing public debt slow and hard to control.

  • Fragmented management

    Registration, payments, installments and collection lived in separate applications, each with its own login. There was no single view of the debt.

  • External dependency

    The legacy was maintained by another agency, on an old platform that was hard to evolve. Some operations depended on tickets, and any improvement was out of the users' control.

  • Manual processes

    Calculations done outside the system, no simulation, documents put together by hand and paper processes made the work slow and error-prone.

  • Little traceability

    It was hard to understand the status of a debt and follow its stages, with no clear history or reports.

Legacy system: each piece of debt information on a different terminal screen.

Who uses it

In the public debt department, each public servant has different responsibilities, and what each one can do in the system is defined by their access profile. To understand the pain points of the team as a whole, I talked to the department's coordination, who closely follow the servants' work and gather the problems they report day to day.

Public debt servant

  • Uses the system every day and only accesses the features enabled for their profile
  • Knows the public debt rules well
  • Serves taxpayers, in person or at the virtual desk, who want to negotiate or understand the status of their debts

Goals

  • Give the taxpayer a clear answer on the spot
  • Resolve each case without depending on other systems or another agency
  • Be confident that amounts and documents are correct

Needs

  • Quickly find the debt data
  • See everything about it in one place
  • Calculate amounts and payment terms in the system itself
  • Generate ready-made documents, without rework

Pain points

  • Information scattered across several applications
  • Not being able to tell the taxpayer how much they'll pay
  • Putting documents together by copying data from screens
  • Depending on tickets for operations like offsetting

What they say

The pain points in the team's own words.

  • Information scattered across several systems

    “If I only want to access the debt system, I type ‘debt’ here and enter my username and password. If I want to look at payments, then I open another application. […] To see installments, that's yet another application.”

  • Not being able to negotiate with the taxpayer

    “No, the system doesn't let you see when they can pay. […] There are several rules […] the system doesn't show, there's no screen for it. So you can't say how much the installment will be, for example, the taxpayer has to look at their own situation and do the math themselves.”

  • Documents put together by hand

    “Since the system doesn't provide it, we just copy and paste […] and then we have to format it. Usually we send the one from the [taxpayer portal]. But sometimes they want something more detailed that isn't there.”

  • Depending on another agency

    “For offsetting, we don't have access. We keep opening tickets. […] Sometimes it says it's being offset, but it actually isn't.”

Design system

The system is built in Angular, so I based most components on the PrimeNG library the team already used. That way, development didn't have to build components from scratch. For needs the library didn't cover, I created specific components, and I created the entire token structure, which is what gives the system its own identity on top of the library.

The design system evolved and was documented throughout the project, and today it's the reference handed over to development.

Standardization

Every screen uses the same page layout component: side menu, top bar, page header and content area. On top of that base, most modules repeat the same screen types: search (list with filters), view (header and tabs), create or edit (form).

  • For users

    Learning one module means learning them all. Actions and information are always in the same place.

  • For the team

    Developers implemented the structure once and reused it in other modules.

  • For the project

    It's what made it possible to deliver around 370 screens consistently.

Solution

The whole debt cycle in one system. The MVP has 21 modules, organized into five major areas.

  • Records and registration

    7 modules

    From registration to the debt's full history.

    • Registration
    • Taxpayer
    • Debt source
    • Revenue types and products
    • Debt stages
    • Electronic ledger
    • Statute of limitations

  • Payment and negotiation

    6 modules

    Simulating and recording payments, agreements and guarantees.

    • Lump-sum payment
    • Installments
    • Offsetting
    • Deduction methods
    • Payment slips
    • Guarantees

  • Calculation

    3 modules

    Updating amounts with configurable rules.

    • Interest and inflation adjustment
    • Adjustment indexes
    • Charges and fees

  • Management

    3 modules

    Control, access and monitoring.

    • Reports
    • Users and permissions
    • Authentication

  • Collection

    2 modules

    Out-of-court collection actions.

    • Protest
    • Defaulter registry

Highlighted flows

Registration data

Search accepts any debtor data. There are many possible search fields: if they stayed fixed at the top, they would push the list down, even when the analyst only wants to search by a registration number. That's why they went into a side panel.

Tabs inside the registration instead of separate pages. People switch topics without losing the debtor's context. I ordered the tabs by frequency of use.

Statements, debt certificates (CDA) and installment agreements also come out ready from the system, without copying and formatting data from screens.

From what I saw of the team's routine, edits and new records would still be done on the computer. So I used graceful degradation: in 17 of the 21 modules, mobile only has viewing and history, for quick lookups when the need comes up.

Payment simulation

Taxpayers went to the Attorney General's Office in person to ask how much they would pay in a lump sum, with a discount from a public notice, or in installments. The old system didn't do this calculation, and the taxpayer was often told to do the math on their own or to look for another system, after having already traveled there.

Deductions are split by type, lump sum, installments and offsetting, with each one's status, balance and days overdue. The simulation starts here.

Now the simulation happens on a single screen. The analyst selects the registrations, chooses the payment type and the discount, and the system calculates the rest.

The summary shows the original amount and the discounted amount. The simulation can be downloaded for the taxpayer or turned into an installment request.

Next steps

  1. 01

    Follow users as they use the system, to gather real feedback.

  2. 02

    Run usability tests after launch, starting with viewing a registration, the most used flow. Then the payment simulation, measuring how many taxpayer inquiries turn into deductions.

  3. 03

    Improve the flow and usability of some form screens, to make the forms less dense.

Redesigning the Meu Ônibus public transit app

A redesign focused on rethinking the whole experience to meet users' real needs.

  • Mobile
  • User research
My role
UX/UI Designer
Type
Personal project
Timeline
3 months

What I did

I ran the user research, created the persona and the journey map, and redesigned the app's flows and screens, from sketches to final screens.

Context

Meu Ônibus ("My Bus") is the public transit app of Fortaleza, built to bring together two everyday tasks for bus riders in one place.

What the app does

  • Bus times at stops

    Shows in real time when each bus arrives at the stop.

  • Card top-up

    Tops up the transit card without going to a service point.

Main problems

  • Usability issues

    Over time, the flows became confusing, the information poorly organized and the interface unintuitive.

  • Two apps

    The features were split: one app to check and top up the cards and another one just for bus times.

This is an unofficial redesign, made as a study. It has no connection with the app or with the people who build it.

Research

I use the app every day, so I started with my own experience. Then I checked whether other users went through the same.

Store reviews

I analyzed the top reviews of both apps on the App Store and Play Store. I kept the ones that explained where users got stuck and discarded vague ones, like "the app is bad" or "it freezes too much".

"When it opens, it could quickly show the bus stops and their times, but it takes several taps to see the information."

1.0 · helpful to 317 people

"Checking the balance is complicated, it takes several taps, and the font color doesn't contrast well with the background."

1.0 · helpful to 159 people

In-person observation

5 people used the app's main features while I watched, following this script:

  1. What is it like to check the bus time?
  2. What is it like to check the transit card balance?
  3. What is it like to top up the card?

In purple, the answers. In green, the ideas that came up.

Persona

I created the persona from the research, mainly from the in-person sessions. It helped me decide which problems to prioritize.

Ana Carolina, 20 years old

Advertising student in Fortaleza · lives far from downtown · takes the bus to college every day

"It's so frustrating when the app says the bus is coming and it takes another 20 minutes! (...) Imagine getting on the bus and finding out there's no credit on your card!"

Goals

  • get to and from college quickly, making the most of her time
  • have reliable times and routes to plan her trip
  • top up her card easily, without going to a service point

Frustrations

  • needs several taps to see her card balance
  • has to leave the app and open another one to top up
  • has trusted the prediction before and the bus never showed up or took too long

Behaviors

  • uses the app every day to check predictions and plan her trip
  • prefers apps that save time and simplify her routine

Journey map

What the user does, thinks and feels at each stage, and what could be better.

Main problems

After the whole research phase, I could define which pain points mattered most.

  1. 01.  Balance and top-up

    Checking the balance takes several taps, and the top-up flow is slow and confusing.

  2. 02.  Split apps

    To top up, the user has to leave Meu Ônibus and open another app.

  3. 03.  Usability and navigation

    Routes that are hard to find, a confusing interface and too many taps.

  4. 04.  Design and accessibility

    Low contrast and text in a color close to the background, hard to read for older people and people with low vision.

Wireframes

I sketched the new flows by hand before moving to Figma.

  1. 01.  Fewer taps in the old flow

    I revisited the old flow to shorten it and make tasks simpler.

  2. 02.  Balance and top-up first

    I prioritized checking the balance and topping up: seeing the balance took several taps, and topping up required another app.

Result

The before and after of each flow.

01  Home

Before
  • When you open the app, the map shows up far from the city. You have to zoom into the circle to see nearby stops.
  • Once zoomed in, there are so many markers that the map gets confusing.

01  Home

After

The app opens right at the nearest stop, with the lines that stop there and how long until each bus arrives.

The main card's balance sits at the top of the home screen, with zero taps.

02  Search

Before
  • Search fails if the right filter isn't chosen first. I searched for the line number with the "Local" filter selected and found nothing.
  • The search field disappears while typing.
  • After choosing a direction, you have to search everything again to switch.
  • Contrast is poor, and the map markers open the same screen under any filter.

02  Search

After
  • Lines, stops and trip became tabs of the same search, and each one shows suggestions before you type.
  • On the Trip tab, the user enters where they are and where they're going and sees the line combinations with the total time.

02.1  Search results

After
  • The line shows the full route, and you can switch direction on the same screen.
  • The stop shows every line that stops there and how long until each one arrives.
  • The trip shows the route on the map, the arrival time and where to get off.

03  Balance and top-up

Before
  • To top up, the user left Meu Ônibus and had to use another app.
  • Even in the top-up app, the balance was hidden among several buttons.
  • You had to choose the card before seeing the balance, one extra tap.
  • The balance, the most sought-after information, was at the bottom of the screen.

03  Wallet

After

The wallet brings the transit cards together, each with its balance and a top-up button.

The user picks the card they check most, and its balance shows up on the home screen.

03.1  Top-up

After

The whole top-up flow on a single screen, inside Meu Ônibus: amount, payment method and confirmation.

Auto top-up: the user saves a credit card, and the transit card is topped up automatically when the balance drops below one fare.

What changed in using the app

TaskBeforeAfter
Check the balanceAt least 3 to 4 steps, in another app, with the balance at the bottom of the screenOn the home screen, with zero taps
Top up the cardLeave Meu Ônibus and use another appOn a single screen, inside the app, with an auto top-up option
Find the nearest stopZoom into the map until you find the stopsThe app opens at the nearest stop, with each bus's time
Search for a lineFailed without the right filter, and the field disappeared while typingTabs for lines, stops and trip, with suggestions before typing

Conclusion

Listening to and watching other users was essential to propose solutions that hit the mark, before assuming anything.

Photo booth for a design event

I created an AI-powered photo booth experience, from the visual concept to a working product, in a single day and without writing a line of code.

  • Design with AI
  • Volunteer project
My role
Idea, flow, UX/UI and directing the development with AI
Tools
Figma, Claude, VS Code, Supabase and GitHub Pages
Project type
Volunteer work

Goal

I went to an event held in the same venue where our community event would take place a week later. There was a single-person study booth there, and we thought: why not use this space to make a photo booth for our event?

  1. 01

    Give attendees a different and fun way to capture the event.

  2. 02

    Every story posted would carry the event's visual identity and @uxfor.br.

Challenges

  • Quick to use, no instructions

    People walk in, take their photos and leave. The app had to be easy and intuitive.

  • Sending to the phone

    The photos had to leave the laptop and reach each person's phone, ready to post.

  • No budget

    A volunteer project, with no budget for paid hosting tools.

Process

01. I started with the problem

Before any screen, I wrote to the AI as if it were a briefing: the setting (a laptop in a booth, during an event), who would use it, what happened step by step and what people took home at the end. With that context, the AI had less to guess and asked about what was missing.

What I said

i want to build an app for the following scenario:

  • i'll leave my laptop in a booth during an event so people can go in and take photos
  • when they enter the booth they click take photo
  • the screen should behave like a real photo booth
  1. a 3 second countdown shows up so the person can get ready for the photo
  2. this repeats 3 times (3 photos)
  3. after taking the 3 photos, the system shows a qr code on the screen that can be scanned with a phone
  4. when the qr code is opened on the phone, the photos show up inside the template i'll make (a template ready for instagram stories) and the separate photos too (individual photos)

my questions before starting this project:

  1. how do i connect the system running on the web with the phone
  2. can i keep the app local only? or would it be better to host it somewhere? (i thought about github)

What Claude answered

Based on my prompt, it walked me through the whole solution architecture: the initial flow, how to connect the web booth to the phone through a QR code, hosting the app, storing the photos in the cloud and making them available, plus anticipating technical limits, risks and error states. All of that before writing any code.

02. Design in Figma

Based on the flow Claude gave me, I designed the waiting screen, the camera, the QR code screen and the phone page with the event's visual identity. Then I sent the frame links to the AI and asked it to build following that layout.

03. Deciding how to build it

I didn't know the tools, so for each decision I asked the AI to show me the options and what changed with each one. The final call was mine.

DecisionChoiceWhy
Where to write the codeA folder in VS Code, with HTML, CSS and JavaScript, no frameworkA small site I could open and test on my own
Where to store the photosSupabaseFree, with enough storage for the event.
Where to publishGitHub PagesFree and with https, which the camera requires

04. Development

I connected the project folder to Claude, and it created and edited the files there. My job was a loop: ask, test in VS Code and come back saying what was right and what had to change. In Supabase, I sent a screenshot of each step whenever I got stuck. I learned what a bucket is, what an access rule is for and which key can stay in the code.

05. Testing and bugs

Nothing came out perfect the first time. For each bug, I told the AI what happened, where it happened and sent a screenshot.

BugWhat happenedHow it was fixed
Filter on SafariBlack and white photos were saved in colorThe filter started being applied to the image itself, pixel by pixel
Grain on the phoneIn the camera preview, the filter moved around and was too strongThe effect was redone to stay still and lighter
Broken imagesSome of the template drawings didn't show upThe SVG files were simplified

06. Iterations

During the event: photos from the phone

I noticed some people might not go to the booth but would still want the template. So the site started working on phones too, and I spread QR codes to the site around the venue.

After the event: photos from the gallery

People wanted to keep using the template with photos they had already taken. So, before the camera, I added a screen with two options: take a photo or choose from the gallery. Choosing the gallery puts the selected photos into the story templates.

Result

50+

Photo sessions at the event. Each session is someone who took (or chose) photos and got the story template ready, with the UXFor identity.

What I learned

  1. 01

    AI writes the code, but the design decisions are still mine. I was the one who saw what to build, how each screen should be, when something was wrong and what to change after people used it.

  2. 02

    A good prompt looks like a briefing. Setting, who uses it, what happens and the expected result. The more context, the less the AI has to guess.

  3. 03

    Test and fix after every answer.

  4. 04

    The idea worked because it was a simple project. It was a small site, with no login and no payments. If there were sensitive data, someone who knows code would need to review what the AI wrote, especially for security.
