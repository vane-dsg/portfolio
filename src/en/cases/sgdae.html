    Context

    The public debt registry gathers amounts that were not paid to the state and are then collected by the Attorney General's Office. It accounts for an important share of the state's revenue.

    How it was

    All management ran on a mainframe system, built and maintained by another state agency and split into several terminal applications.

    Goal

    Replace the legacy with a system owned by the Attorney General's Office, bringing the full debt cycle into one place.

    Methodology

    The system has many flows, and each one went through the same cycle within the sprints, with meetings twice a week. The deadline was tight, so stakeholders only saw high-fidelity, prototyped screens.

    1. 01

      Planning

      We defined which module would be designed in the sprint.

    2. 02

      Understand the problem

      Meeting with the stakeholders and conversations with subject matter experts.

    3. 03

      High fidelity

      I designed the screens straight in high fidelity, already prototyped.

    4. 04

      Review

      In the next meeting, corrections and change requests to the flow came up.

    5. 05

      Adjustments

      I made the adjustments and brought the new version to the next meeting, until the flow was closed.

    The cycle repeated for every module.

    My notebook

    Diagrams and journey maps weren't an expected deliverable, but I needed them to understand the rules of each module and user story. So I did everything in my notebook, during and after the meetings.

    Problem

    What made managing public debt slow and hard to control.

    • Fragmented management

      Registration, payments, installments and collection lived in separate applications, each with its own login. There was no single view of the debt.

    • External dependency

      The legacy was maintained by another agency, on an old platform that was hard to evolve. Some operations depended on tickets, and any improvement was out of the users' control.

    • Manual processes

      Calculations done outside the system, no simulation, documents put together by hand and paper processes made the work slow and error-prone.

    • Little traceability

      It was hard to understand the status of a debt and follow its stages, with no clear history or reports.

    Legacy system: each piece of debt information on a different terminal screen.

    Who uses it

    In the public debt department, each public servant has different responsibilities, and what each one can do in the system is defined by their access profile. To understand the pain points of the team as a whole, I talked to the department's coordination, who closely follow the servants' work and gather the problems they report day to day.

    Public debt servant

    • Uses the system every day and only accesses the features enabled for their profile
    • Knows the public debt rules well
    • Serves taxpayers, in person or at the virtual desk, who want to negotiate or understand the status of their debts

    Goals

    • Give the taxpayer a clear answer on the spot
    • Resolve each case without depending on other systems or another agency
    • Be confident that amounts and documents are correct

    Needs

    • Quickly find the debt data
    • See everything about it in one place
    • Calculate amounts and payment terms in the system itself
    • Generate ready-made documents, without rework

    Pain points

    • Information scattered across several applications
    • Not being able to tell the taxpayer how much they'll pay
    • Putting documents together by copying data from screens
    • Depending on tickets for operations like offsetting

    What they say

    The pain points in the team's own words.

    • Information scattered across several systems

      “If I only want to access the debt system, I type ‘debt’ here and enter my username and password. If I want to look at payments, then I open another application. […] To see installments, that's yet another application.”

    • Not being able to negotiate with the taxpayer

      “No, the system doesn't let you see when they can pay. […] There are several rules […] the system doesn't show, there's no screen for it. So you can't say how much the installment will be, for example, the taxpayer has to look at their own situation and do the math themselves.”

    • Documents put together by hand

      “Since the system doesn't provide it, we just copy and paste […] and then we have to format it. Usually we send the one from the [taxpayer portal]. But sometimes they want something more detailed that isn't there.”

    • Depending on another agency

      “For offsetting, we don't have access. We keep opening tickets. […] Sometimes it says it's being offset, but it actually isn't.”

    Design system

    The system is built in Angular, so I based most components on the PrimeNG library the team already used. That way, development didn't have to build components from scratch. For needs the library didn't cover, I created specific components, and I created the entire token structure, which is what gives the system its own identity on top of the library.

    The design system evolved and was documented throughout the project, and today it's the reference handed over to development.

    Standardization

    Every screen uses the same page layout component: side menu, top bar, page header and content area. On top of that base, most modules repeat the same screen types: search (list with filters), view (header and tabs), create or edit (form).

    • For users

      Learning one module means learning them all. Actions and information are always in the same place.

    • For the team

      Developers implemented the structure once and reused it in other modules.

    • For the project

      It's what made it possible to deliver around 370 screens consistently.

    Solution

    The whole debt cycle in one system. The MVP has 21 modules, organized into five major areas.

    • Records and registration

      7 modules

      From registration to the debt's full history.

      • Registration
      • Taxpayer
      • Debt source
      • Revenue types and products
      • Debt stages
      • Electronic ledger
      • Statute of limitations

    • Payment and negotiation

      6 modules

      Simulating and recording payments, agreements and guarantees.

      • Lump-sum payment
      • Installments
      • Offsetting
      • Deduction methods
      • Payment slips
      • Guarantees

    • Calculation

      • Adjustment indexes
      • Charges and fees

    • Management

      3 modules

    Highlighted flows

    Registration data

    Search accepts any debtor data. There are many possible search fields: if they stayed fixed at the top, they would push the list down, even when the analyst only wants to search by a registration number. That's why they went into a side panel.

    Tabs inside the registration instead of separate pages. People switch topics without losing the debtor's context. I ordered the tabs by frequency of use.

    Statements, debt certificates (CDA) and installment agreements also come out ready from the system, without copying and formatting data from screens.

    From what I saw of the team's routine, edits and new records would still be done on the computer. So I used graceful degradation: in 17 of the 21 modules, mobile only has viewing and history, for quick lookups when the need comes up.

    Payment simulation

    Taxpayers went to the Attorney General's Office in person to ask how much they would pay in a lump sum, with a discount from a public notice, or in installments. The old system didn't do this calculation, and the taxpayer was often told to do the math on their own or to look for another system, after having already traveled there.

    Deductions are split by type, lump sum, installments and offsetting, with each one's status, balance and days overdue. The simulation starts here.

    Now the simulation happens on a single screen. The analyst selects the registrations, chooses the payment type and the discount, and the system calculates the rest.

    The summary shows the original amount and the discounted amount. The simulation can be downloaded for the taxpayer or turned into an installment request.
